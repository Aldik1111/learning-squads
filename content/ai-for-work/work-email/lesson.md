# Draft an email without inventing commitments

An email draft should help a reader take the intended next step. More confident wording is not always more accurate wording. Start with the facts you are allowed to state, the intended audience, and the action you want to request.

## Turn a brief into a bounded request

Fictional brief: "A project outline is ready for review. The reviewer is Jordan. Ask whether a review by Thursday is possible. No deadline has been agreed." A useful instruction is: "Draft a friendly email under 100 words. Ask Jordan whether Thursday works. Do not say Jordan has already committed. Include a subject and one clear next step."

A safe candidate might read: "Subject: Outline review timing. Hi Jordan, the outline is ready for your review. Would you be able to share comments by Thursday? If another date works better, please let me know. Thanks."

Compare that with "Thanks for agreeing to finish by Thursday." The second draft invents agreement. The distinction is easy to miss because the sentence sounds ordinary. Check names, dates, attachments, promises, and requests against the brief. A draft mentioning an attachment also needs a real attachment before sending.

## Practice

Use this fictional brief: "The workshop slides need feedback. Ask Morgan for comments on clarity. Friday is a proposed date, not an agreed deadline. Do not promise any discount or service." Write your prompt and a short draft. Then mark two details you verified and one phrase you revised to avoid an invented commitment.

You can draft manually; using an AI tool is optional. Do not paste real customer information or confidential messages into an unapproved tool. This exercise creates a draft only: a human reviews and sends any actual message. Submit the fictional draft and your review notes as the practical result.
